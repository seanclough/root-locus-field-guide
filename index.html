The one idea underneath every rule

A closed-loop pole is any s where 1 + K G(s) = 0, which means K G(s) = −1. For K > 0 that splits into two conditions:

∠G(s) = Σ∠(s − zj) − Σ∠(s − pi) = ±180°   and   K = 1 / |G(s)|

The angle condition doesn't involve K, so it alone decides where the locus is. The magnitude condition then tells you which K puts a pole at a given point. Every sketching rule below is a shortcut for the angle condition.

Drag the red test point around. Each zero adds its angle, each pole subtracts its angle. When the total hits ±180°, the point is on the locus. Get within a few pixels of a branch and it snaps on. The faint background lines are contours of constant ∠G, every 15°; the locus is the 180° contour.

Drag the test point.

Notice how the contours pile up around poles and zeros. Every angle contour passes through every pole and zero, which is why branches start and end there.

The rules, one plot each

Rule numbers follow the lecture order. Construction marks are in red pencil.

Rule 1

Branches start at poles and end at zeros

There's one branch per closed-loop pole, so np branches. Each starts on an open-loop pole at K = 0 and ends on an open-loop zero as K → ∞. Branches with no finite zero to land on, np − nz of them, run off to infinity. The whole picture is mirrored across the real axis, because complex roots of a real polynomial come in conjugate pairs.

Why: the closed-loop characteristic equation is D(s) + K N(s) = 0. At K = 0 it's just D = 0. Divide by K and let K → ∞ and it becomes N = 0, which has only nz roots; the rest escape to infinity.

Dots show the closed-loop poles at gains that double from one dot to the next. They crowd together near the poles and spread out as K grows. Arrows point toward increasing K.

Rule 2

Real-axis segments: left of an odd count

A point on the real axis is on the locus when the number of real poles plus real zeros to its right is odd. Complex pairs never count. The numbers above the axis show that count for each stretch, and the odd stretches are shaded.

Why: from a real test point, every real pole or zero to its left is seen at 0°, every one to its right at 180°, and a conjugate pair at +θ and −θ, which cancel. The total is 180° (mod 360°) only when an odd number of 180°'s add up.

Rules 3 & 4

Asymptotes and their centroid

The np − nz branches that go to infinity approach straight lines. Their angles depend only on the excess np − nz, and they all start from one point on the real axis.

φi = 180°(2i + 1) / (np − nz)     σa = (Σpi − Σzj) / (np − nz)

Why: from far away, the whole cluster looks like np − nz poles stacked at one point, the centroid (poles weigh +1, zeros −1). A distant point at angle θ then sees a total angle of −(np − nz)θ. Set that equal to 180°(2i + 1) and solve for θ.

Note that the centroid can sit to the right of every pole (the lecture example has σa = +1/3). Asymptotes are guides for large K, not part of the locus.

Rule 5

Breakaway and break‑in: hills and valleys of K

Every point σ on the real axis comes with a price: the gain it would take to put a closed-loop pole exactly there. Solve 1 + K N/D = 0 for K:

K(σ) = −D(σ) / N(σ)     here   K(σ) = −σ(σ + 1) / (σ + 3)

The chart under the locus plots that price against σ, lined up with the plot above it, for . To read it, draw a horizontal line at the current K (the red line). Every place it crosses the solid curve is a real closed-loop pole. Raising K slides the line up. The curve dives toward K = 0 at each pole and shoots up to ∞ at the zero, which is Rule 1 showing up again.

Between two poles the curve is a hill. The price is 0 at both poles, so it has to rise and come back down. As K grows, the two poles climb opposite sides of the hill toward each other and meet at the top. That peak is the breakaway. Once the line is above it, no real point on the segment has a big enough gain, so the poles have to leave the axis as a complex pair.

Between two zeros, or a zero and −∞, the curve is a valley. The price is infinite at both ends. The rising line first touches the valley at its floor, and that's where the complex pair lands: the break‑in. After that the line cuts the valley twice, and the two poles slide apart, one toward the zero and one toward −∞.

Between a pole and a zero the curve usually climbs straight from 0 to ∞, so no two poles ever collide there.

Why dK/ds = 0. A hilltop or a valley floor is where the slope is zero. That's all the formula says:

dK/ds = 0  ⇔  N(s)D′(s) − N′(s)D(s) = 0

Here dK/ds = −(s² + 6s + 3)/(s + 3)², which is zero at s = −3 ± √6. That gives the hilltop at −0.551 (K = 5 − 2√6 = 0.101) and the valley floor at −5.449 (K = 5 + 2√6 = 9.90). The lecture method of tabulating K at a few σ and looking for the largest value is reading this chart by hand.

Where did K go? There's no solid curve anywhere between K = 0.101 and 9.90. Those gains still have closed-loop poles; they just aren't on the real axis. The dashed line fills the gap with the pair's shared real part, which here is exactly −(1 + K)/2. It runs from the hilltop to the valley floor, so every K is accounted for: two crossings on solid curves, or one on the dashed line.

Why the solid curve has gaps. Over the hatched stretches, −D/N comes out negative, so no positive gain puts a pole there. That's Rule 2 again, reached from the other direction.

Why the pair leaves at ±90°: near a double root s₀ at gain K₀, the poles move like s − s₀ ≈ ±√(c(K − K₀)) for some real c. On one side of K₀ that square root is real (two real poles); on the other it's imaginary, so the pair moves straight up and down. With two poles and one zero, the off-axis part is an exact circle centered on the zero, radius √6 here.

Rule 6

Departure and arrival angles

A branch leaves a complex pole (or arrives at a complex zero) at an angle you get by applying the angle condition right next to it. Measure the angles from every other pole and zero to that point:

θD = 180° − (Σθp − Σθz)     θA = (Σθp − Σθz) − 180°

The tabs above each plot swap in other transfer functions, so you can see how moving or adding a pole or zero changes the angle.

Why: a test point a hair away from the pole sees all the other poles and zeros at the same angles they see the pole at. The only unknown left in the angle condition is the angle from the pole itself, which is the direction the branch leaves in.

Rule 8

Gain at a point: the magnitude condition

Once you've picked a point s on the locus (say, where it meets a damping-ratio line), the gain that puts a closed-loop pole there is a ratio of vector lengths:

K = Π|s − pi| / Π|s − zj|   (no zeros: denominator = 1)

0.50

The ζ line sits at cos⁻¹ζ from the negative real axis (60° for ζ = 0.5, about 15% overshoot; 90°, the jω axis, for ζ = 0; 0°, the real axis, for ζ = 1). Drag either dashed line, or its round handle, to swing it; the slider, the point, the vectors and K all follow.

Bonus rule

The poles' center of gravity stays put

When there are at least two more poles than zeros (np − nz ≥ 2), the closed-loop poles always add up to the same number, whatever K is:

Σ (closed-loop poles) = Σpi  for every K    (when np − nz ≥ 2)

Imaginary parts cancel in conjugate pairs, so this is really about real parts. Divide by np and it says the average position of the closed-loop poles never moves. Picture each pole as an equal weight on a beam: as K grows the weights slide around, but the balance point stays where it started. So if some poles move left, others must move right by the same total.

Why: the roots of any monic polynomial sn + an−1sn−1 + … add up to −an−1. In D(s) + K N(s), the numerator N has degree nz. If nz ≤ n − 2, then K N never reaches the sn−1 term, so that coefficient comes from D alone and can't depend on K. If nz = n − 1, it does reach it: the coefficient becomes an−1 + K, and the sum slides left as K grows (last tab).

Reading the strip. Under the plot, each row is one branch, in the same color as on the plot. The × is where it started (an open-loop pole) and the dot is the real part of its closed-loop pole now. The red triangle under the beam is the average of all the dots: the balance point.

What it's good for

  • A sanity check. A sketch where every branch drifts left is wrong. In K/[s(s + 2)(s + 4)], the pole running off toward −∞ has to be paid for, and the complex pair pays by drifting right, across the jω axis at K = 48.
  • The last pole for free. At the Rule 8 design point (ζ = 0.5, K = 8.30) the pair sits at −0.667 ± j1.155. The third pole has to be at −6 − 2(−0.667) = −4.667, with no cubic to solve. It's 7 times farther left than the pair, so treating the system as second order is reasonable.
  • Where the centroid comes from. As K → ∞, nz poles settle onto the zeros and the other np − nz spread out symmetrically around one point. For the total to stay Σp, that point has to be (Σp − Σz)/(np − nz), which is Rule 4's σa.

Edge cases

These are the ones that break a quick sketch: multiple roots, breakaway points off the axis, poles on the axis, right-half-plane poles and zeros, and the 0° locus.

Try

    Curves are traced by solving D(s) + K N(s) = 0 at a few thousand gains and following each root. Breakaway points, crossings and angles are computed from the polynomials, not measured.

    Sketching checklist

    The order that keeps a hand sketch honest.

    1. Write the characteristic equation as 1 + K N(s)/D(s) = 0 with N and D monic (leading coefficient +1). If a minus sign is stuck to K, or the thing you're varying isn't a gain, rearrange first.
    2. Plot poles × and zeros ○. Count branches (np) and how many go to infinity (np − nz).
    3. Shade the real-axis segments that have an odd count to their right.
    4. Draw the asymptotes from the centroid.
    5. On segments between two poles (or two zeros), find the breakaway (break-in) point with dK/ds = 0, or just look for the peak of K(σ).
    6. Compute departure angles from complex poles and arrival angles at complex zeros.
    7. Find jω crossings and the critical gain with Routh on D + K N.
    8. Join it all smoothly, symmetric about the real axis. Branches only touch each other at breakaway or break-in points. Use the magnitude condition to label the gain at any point you care about.
